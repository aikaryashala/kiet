A video is a stack of matrices

Play with one tiny 16 × 12 clip all the way down to its numbers, and back up again. Five steps: see it move, open the matrix, hit the wall, encode, decode.

16 × 12 px · 12 frames · one bouncing ball
STEP 01 / THE ILLUSION

Nothing here is moving

Flip a deck of drawings fast enough and a ball bounces. Slow enough and it's twelve separate pictures. Where exactly does one become the other?

Playback

Drop the rate to 1 and the deck falls apart into stills. Push it back up and your visual system stitches them together. The video file never stored motion — it stored twelve pictures and a rate.

Trade-off

More frames per second buys smoother motion and costs proportionally more storage. 24 fps is cinema's old compromise; 60 fps is a game's. Nobody picks a number because it looks nice — they pick the cheapest rate that still fools the eye.

STEP 02 / THE MATRIX

One frame is a table of numbers

A screen has no idea what a ball is. It gets told, cell by cell, how much red, green and blue to emit. So what does a picture look like from the machine's side?

Frame 6 — click any pixel

Three matrices stacked — one per colour channel. Toggle a single channel and the picture collapses into pure intensity: the shape survives, the colour doesn't.

So an image is height × width × 3 numbers, each 0–255, each one byte. A video is that same block repeated once per frame. Everything from here on is a fight with the size of this block.

Ask the room

A greyscale photo needs one number per pixel instead of three. Why do we still use three for almost everything — and where is greyscale still the right call?

STEP 03 / THE PROBLEM

Stored honestly, a film is a terabyte

Our toy clip is 16 × 12. Real ones aren't. Before anyone invents a clever trick, work out what the naive answer actually costs.

Nothing about that gap comes from better hardware. It comes from noticing that the raw block is saying the same thing many times: neighbouring pixels are nearly identical, and so are neighbouring frames. Redundancy is the whole opening.

Trade-off

Squeezing 280:1 means throwing detail away and spending CPU time on every single frame, twice — once to encode, once to play. Storage got cheap; the compromise moved to battery and latency, not disk.

That's the codec in one sentence: keep the occasional whole picture — a keyframe — and between them write only the differences. Coarser colour levels make more pixels count as "identical", which is exactly why the two sliders above pull on the same rope.

Trade-off

Fewer keyframes means a smaller file and worse seeking — jump to the middle of a clip and the player must replay every difference since the last full picture. That pause when you drag a video's scrubber is this trade-off, out loud.

STEP 05 / DECODING

The player rebuilds the matrix

The file no longer holds twelve pictures. It holds one picture and eleven lists of corrections. Watch the decoder put the frames back together — then take one correction away.

Decoder

Drop one packet and the error doesn't vanish next frame — the ball smears until the next keyframe wipes the buffer clean. That's the ghosting you've seen on a stalling stream, and it's evidence the decoder is holding one matrix in memory and patching it in place.

Ask the room

Pair up. One of you is the encoder and may only speak in instructions like "row 4, pixel 9, now rust". The other holds the grid and edits it. Send frames 5 through 8. Then argue about which instruction was the most wasteful — and design a shorter one.

The round trip

Light → sensor → matrix of numbers → quantised → differences → bitstream → file. Then straight back: bitstream → differences → matrix → screen → light. Encoding and decoding are the same map, walked in opposite directions.
